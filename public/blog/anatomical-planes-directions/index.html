Anatomical planes and directions: the language of the body

A practical guide to anatomical position, body planes, and directional terms for medical, nursing, and allied-health students.

Updated September 21, 2026 · 7 minute read · Educational reference

If anatomy feels like a new language, start here. Anatomical planes and directional terms give every lecture, practical, scan, and clinical handover a shared coordinate system. Once the vocabulary is automatic, structures such as the heart, kidney, and trachea become easier to locate and compare.

Fast memory rule: describe a structure from the patient’s standard anatomical position, not from the direction you are looking at the image.

What is the anatomical position?

The anatomical position is the reference posture used to describe the human body: standing upright, head and eyes facing forward, arms beside the body, palms facing forward, and feet directed forward. The terms “left” and “right” refer to the person being described, not the observer.

That convention matters when you read “the heart is left of the midline” or “the spleen lies in the left upper quadrant.” It keeps descriptions consistent even when the person is lying down, the image is mirrored, or the body is rotated.

The three major anatomical planes

PlaneWhat it dividesUseful example
SagittalLeft and right portionsA midsagittal view can show the brain, spinal cord, and nasal cavity in profile.
Coronal (frontal)Anterior and posterior portionsA coronal view helps compare the two lungs or the right and left kidneys.
Transverse (axial)Superior and inferior portionsAn axial CT slice shows relationships across the thorax or abdomen.

Planes are imaginary reference surfaces. They are not cuts that always follow an organ’s own shape. In imaging, “axial,” “coronal,” and “sagittal” describe how the data has been reconstructed; in gross anatomy, the same words describe how a specimen or model is sectioned.

Directional terms you need first

Anterior and posterior

Anterior means toward the front of the body; posterior means toward the back. The sternum is anterior to the heart, while the vertebral column is posterior to it.

Superior and inferior

Superior means closer to the head; inferior means closer to the feet. The thorax is superior to the abdomen, and the pelvis is inferior to the abdomen.

Medial and lateral

Medial means closer to the midline; lateral means farther from it. The nose is medial to the eyes, while the ears are lateral to the eyes.

Proximal and distal

These terms are used most often for limbs. Proximal means closer to the trunk or point of attachment; distal means farther away. The elbow is distal to the shoulder but proximal to the wrist.

Superficial and deep

Superficial structures are nearer the body surface; deep structures are farther inside. In the thorax, the ribs are superficial to the lungs.

How to use the terms in a practical

  1. State the region first: “In the thorax…”
  2. Choose a landmark: sternum, vertebral column, midline, or a joint.
  3. Describe the relationship: “The heart is posterior to the sternum and medial to the lungs.”
  4. Check your view: rotate the 3D model or switch to a front, side, or back view.

Common exam mistakes

  • Using “left” and “right” from the viewer’s perspective.
  • Confusing coronal with sagittal: coronal is front/back; sagittal is left/right.
  • Calling a structure “above” or “below” when superior or inferior is expected.
  • Using proximal and distal for structures that are not on a limb.
