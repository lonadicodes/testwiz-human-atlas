Neuroanatomy

Brain anatomy: lobes, brainstem, cerebellum, and clinical links

A structured overview for students who need to identify the major brain regions and connect them to neurologic assessment.

Updated September 21, 2026 · 9 minute read · Educational reference

The brain is difficult to learn from a list because its most important relationships are spatial. A useful first pass is to move from outside to inside: cranial cavity and meninges, cerebral hemispheres and lobes, deep structures, brainstem, cerebellum, then the ventricular and vascular relationships that explain common clinical signs.

Study cue: identify a region by location, then state its broad job. Avoid treating a lobe as if it has only one function; most real tasks use networks.

Brainstem and cerebellum

The brainstem includes the midbrain, pons, and medulla. It connects the cerebrum with the spinal cord, carries ascending and descending pathways, and contains nuclei involved in cranial nerve function and vital autonomic control.

The cerebellum sits posterior to the pons and medulla. It fine-tunes timing, coordination, balance, posture, and motor learning. It does not initiate every movement, but it compares intended and ongoing movement so output becomes smoother.

Meninges and cerebrospinal fluid

The brain and spinal cord are covered by three meninges: the tough dura mater, the web-like arachnoid mater, and the delicate pia mater that follows the brain surface. Cerebrospinal fluid circulates through the ventricles and subarachnoid space, cushioning the central nervous system and helping maintain its chemical environment.

  3. Rotate posteriorly to find the cerebellum and brainstem.
  4. Switch to Identify Mode or ask the Entity Q&A questions after naming the region yourself.

Spatial learning is especially valuable in neuroanatomy because many structures are small, curved, and arranged around spaces such as the ventricles. A review of multimedia neuroanatomy teaching also describes the subject as challenging because of complex geometry and spatial relationships. Use 3D as a supplement to your lecturer’s diagrams, imaging, and practical work.

Neurologic assessment connections

  • Level of consciousness gives a fast view of global brain function.
  • Pupil size and reactivity provide information about pathways involving the midbrain and cranial nerves.
  • Speech, facial movement, strength, sensation, coordination, and gait help localise dysfunction across different networks.
  • Always interpret a finding with the full history, examination, and local clinical protocol.

Quick check

Which lobe is most associated with visual processing?

The occipital lobe, although vision also depends on pathways and networks beyond the occipital cortex.

Where is the cerebellum?

It lies posterior to the pons and medulla, beneath the occipital lobes.
